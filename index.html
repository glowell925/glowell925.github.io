Resin Cassette Cleaning Fixture

Mobile powered fixture that lifts, tilts, and rotates a Carbon resin vat for a clean corner pour — Engineering Internship, The Technology House

At The Technology House, a manufacturer in Streetsboro, OH, my supervisor handed me a problem I had already met on the production floor: Carbon's large resin cassettes were being cleaned in place, inside the printer. Resin that escaped had a direct path down into the machine's internals, the job ran about 18 minutes of scooping per machine, and it meant reaching over live equipment. I designed and built a mobile fixture that takes the cassette out of the printer so it can be poured instead of scooped.

The design I threw away

My first design was a fully fabricated steel assembly — telescoping column, yoke, pivot shaft on bearings, index plate and detent pin. It worked on screen. After review with the company's engineers I deleted almost all of it: a sourced motorized drafting-table base already gave powered height and 0–90° tilt, more rigidly and with a fraction of the parts I would have had to fabricate. One purchase replaced nearly every custom component. Knowing when to stop designing was the most useful thing I took away from this project.

  • Corner drain is the whole point. Tilting alone drops a full edge and resin runs off along a line. A 9" turntable mounted above the tilt axis re-clocks the cassette 45° so a single corner points down — rotate while level, then tilt.
  • Reverse-engineered the interface. Measuring Carbon's in-service jack-cart revealed a locating template already in use; I recreated it in SolidWorks rather than inventing my own, which bought jack-cart compatibility and proven stability for free.
  • Made the locating hardware myself — four locking circles and four support cylinders in HP PA 12, printed on the HP Multi Jet Fusion machines I ran daily, then unpacked, blown off, and bead blasted end to end.
  • Drop-in assembly: a 24" × 30" plywood base laser-engraved with the exact position of every printed part, so the layout is reproducible by anyone.
  • Material calls: the stock glass top was swapped for 1" HDPE — resin-resistant and it takes fasteners directly. Nothing touching resin is PLA (it creeps and uncured resin attacks it).
  • Delivered at $861 total ($449 purchased, the balance in-house printing), and presented the design, test results, and the open problem to the engineering team.
Result — and the problem still open

The fixture works. It meets the jack-cart at its fixed height and hands the cassette across cleanly — tested, passed — and lifts, tilts, and swivels under load. The pour test failed: even corner-down, resin runs along the cassette's outside wall and spills onto the base, and a moldable funnel was too narrow to catch it and fouled the interface hardware. My proposed fix — a clip-on spout that carries resin past the cassette body in mid-air — was handed off with the fixture as a concept sketch. Designed, not built.

The rest of the internship: daily production work on both platforms. On Carbon DLS — measuring resin pours to spec, cleaning machines, washing and baking finished parts, and quality control on completed builds. On HP Multi Jet Fusion — printer cleaning and maintenance, extracting parts from cooling units, loading material into build units, bead blasting, and vapor smoothing. Running the machines is why the cassette-cleaning problem was one I had felt myself rather than read in a spec.

Tools: SolidWorks (assembly & mates) · MecAgent CAD Copilot · HP Multi Jet Fusion (HP PA 12) · laser engraving · Carbon DLS · DFM

Rotary Inverted Pendulum (Furuta)

Underactuated nonlinear-controls build — full stack from CAD to control loop, prof-recommended

A from-scratch rotary inverted pendulum, taken on at the recommendation of a robotics/controls research professor at UW–Madison. A motor-driven arm sweeps horizontally while a passive pendulum hangs from its tip; the goal is to swing it up and balance it upright — the classic underactuated, unstable controls benchmark. I'm building the whole stack myself: CAD and 3D printing, sensor and motor-driver wiring, and (next) the real-time control firmware.

Current status — honest

The mechanical and electrical build is complete — printed parts assembled, electronics soldered, motor, driver, and both encoders wired to the Pi. Control firmware is the next milestone — swing-up and LQR balance are designed but not yet written. This page will update as it comes alive.

  • Sensing: AS5600 12-bit magnetic encoder (I²C) on the pendulum; quadrature encoder on the N20 gear motor for the arm.
  • Actuation: L298N H-bridge driving the N20 motor, with a planned software duty ceiling (6 V motor on a 12 V rail).
  • Compute: Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W, targeting a 200–500 Hz control loop.
  • Planned control: energy-based (Åström–Furuta) swing-up handing off to an LQR stabilizer about upright, on a 4-state estimate [θ, θ̇, φ, φ̇].
  • 6 print iterations refining the arm, encoder housing, and pendulum pivot.

Stack: Raspberry Pi (Python) · AS5600 / I²C · N20 quadrature encoder · L298N driver · SolidWorks · FDM printing · control theory (energy shaping, LQR)

Spread Trader Bot

Autonomous options-spread trading system on the Charles Schwab API — built with my father, who runs SmartOptionSeller

An autonomous Python system that trades a live options strategy through the Charles Schwab API. The strategy itself is my father's proprietary playbook — he runs the SmartOptionSeller options newsletter and has traded it by hand for years — so the specifics stay private. My role was to be the AI interpreter: turn a proven manual playbook into a self-running system that scans the market continuously, evaluates candidates against his rules, and routes real orders with no human in the loop.

The hump I had to clear

In simulation the system looked unstoppable — clean, profitable setups available constantly. Live was a different world: nothing would fill. Closing that gap wasn't a code fix, it was a research problem. So I built the instrumentation to answer it: log every attempt, mine the data for what actually executes versus what only looks good on paper, and feed those findings back into the bot. Retuning the midpoint pricing and adding a sell-to-rescue retry loop took daily fill volume from roughly 30 trades to over 140, with a peak day collecting $1,460 in credit. The bot runs live and continues trading daily.

  • Built a data-collection pipeline — every scan logs quotes, candidate trades, submitted prices, and fill outcomes into a structured dataset of what executes vs. what doesn't.
  • Ran the research loop — analyzed that dataset to isolate the conditions and price offsets where real fills land, then translated the findings back into the bot's decision logic.
  • Automated trade logging — a Gmail-to-Excel pipeline captures every executed trade into a clean, analyzable record for ongoing review.
  • Diagnosed an authentication failure that silently killed the live bot ~25 minutes into every run — isolated overlapping trading sessions as the cause after ruling out a suspected library bug — then re-architected to one process, one session.
  • Built position-aware order handling so the bot never sends the broker a conflicting order.

Raspberry Pi Apartment Door Buzzer

Wi-Fi remote door unlock — 3D-printed rack & pinion on a real intercom

I financed, designed, and built a motor-powered opener for my apartment's door buzzer. A 3D-printed, wall-mounted rack-and-pinion physically presses the intercom's unlock button; a Wi-Fi-enabled Raspberry Pi (with a CS roommate on the server side) lets it be triggered from anywhere via a simple URL. A small, satisfying piece of everyday automation that ties together mechanism design, embedded control, and networking.

  • Designed the rack, pinion, and mount to fit an existing TekTone intercom with no modification to the unit.
  • Raspberry Pi GPIO drives the motor; a lightweight web server exposes a trigger endpoint.
  • End-to-end: mechanism → motor → microcontroller → network, remotely actuated.

Stack: Raspberry Pi · Python · 3D printing · rack-and-pinion mechanism · web server
